# Evidence for spaced character reuse

Checked 2026-11-09 using scholarly records, author/repository PDFs and publisher abstracts via CLI. Research findings and design implications only; no curriculum or application specification changes.

## Bottom line

**Spaced practice and repeated retrieval have substantial support. Fibonacci gaps `1,1,2,3,5`, lesson-count equivalents, a revision story every 50 characters, and rereading until no help are not established optimal rules by the studies checked.** Expanding intervals do not consistently outperform equal intervals. Most evidence concerns facts or word pairs, not fluent Mandarin speakers acquiring Chinese literacy.

## What the research actually shows

### Spacing: amount, schedule shape, and retention horizon are different questions

- **Distributed practice generally helps verbal retention.** [Cepeda et al. (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) synthesized 317 experiments (839 assessments, 184 articles). The spacing associated with best retention increased with the final retention delay. Its expanding-versus-fixed comparison was not statistically significant. This is broad memory evidence, not a character-curriculum trial. [Author full text](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.pdf).
- **Calendar time matters, but there is no universal optimum.** [Cepeda et al. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x) tested 1,354 adult completers learning trivia, varying the gap between two learning sessions and testing retention 7–350 days after the second session. For a given retention delay, increasing the gap first helped and then hurt; the best gap shifted with the retention horizon. This experiment varied **one gap**, not a sequence of expanding reviews. Its estimated optimum cannot simply be chained into a schedule. [Author full text](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CVRWP2008.pdf).
- **Expansion itself is not reliably superior.** [Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8), a 29-study meta-analysis, reported a benefit for spaced versus massed retrieval (`g = 0.74`), but no significant average difference between expanding and uniform retrieval spacing (`g = 0.034`). More exposures moderated results toward expansion; this does not validate Fibonacci intervals. These figures are from the publisher abstract, not independently inspected full-text analyses.
- **Separate total spacing from its pattern.** [Karpicke & Bauernschmidt (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023436) studied 96 undergraduates learning Swahili–English pairs. Greater absolute spacing improved recall one week later; expanding, equal and contracting schedules did not differ significantly. Crucially, their intervals counted **intervening experimental trials within a session**, not days or lessons. A null result is not proof that all schedules are equivalent everywhere. [Author full text](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Bauernschmidt_JEPLMC.pdf).

### Retrieval is not the same intervention as rereading

- [Roediger & Karpicke (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x) compared undergraduate prose restudy with free recall without feedback. Restudy performed better after five minutes, but prior retrieval performed better after two days or one week. Immediate ease/confidence and durable retention can diverge. The outcome was recalled passage content, **not decoding or Chinese reading fluency**. [Author full text](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf).
- [Karpicke & Roediger (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408) found that continued testing after the first successful recall of Swahili–English pairs substantially improved week-delayed recall; continued study after success did not improve it in that experiment. This argues against treating one correct response as permanent mastery—not against every use of rereading. It is L2 word-pair evidence, not a direct comparison of Chinese flashcards and story practice. [Author full text](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_Science.pdf).

### Evidence closer to fluent Mandarin speakers learning literacy

- **Unusually close population/task match, weak causal evidence:** [Han & Chen (2010)](https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66838) followed one fluent Mandarin-speaking college student whose Chinese literacy lagged. Twenty two-hour sessions combined audio-assisted rereading until she could read independently, self-selected target words, writing from memory, tutor feedback and dictation. Post-tests showed target-word knowledge, including in studied passages. With **one participant, no control group, multiple combined activities and no long-term retention test**, this supports feasibility, not proof that rereading-to-independence or flashcards caused durable gains. It did not establish transfer to unseen stories. [Repository full text](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5ac42fa7-6e19-45ae-b624-d606b2f6997a/content).
- **Chinese orthographic learning is not simply new vocabulary learning:** [Li, Li & Wang (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2020.104913) studied 67 native Mandarin-speaking second graders learning pseudo-characters through stories or lists. Results supported phonological recoding and contributions from phonetic/semantic radicals to form–sound/meaning associations. This makes connecting written forms to existing spoken knowledge relevant, but does not establish an adult spacing schedule or story superiority. [Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32683144/).
- **Assistance and learning can diverge:** [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.02.025) taught 73 second graders novel compound characters encountered in stories. Both phonetic-radical cues and Zhuyin improved reading accuracy, but only phonetic radicals facilitated measured orthographic learning. This does **not** show that help is harmful or validate a no-help stopping rule. [Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29573645/). Both Li studies were checked at abstract level; children/pseudo-characters differ from adults learning real characters.

## Implications for the proposed authoring approach

These are **design inferences**, not tested prescriptions:

1. **Days versus lessons:** If `1,1,2,3,5` means successive gaps, reviews fall at cumulative offsets `1,2,4,7,12` after introduction. Calendar days and completed lessons coincide only with a regular one-lesson-per-day pace. Irregular study breaks that equivalence. A lesson-based version is a convenient curriculum heuristic, not an evidence-equivalent conversion of elapsed-time experiments.
2. **Static “due characters” helper:** Reasonable for flagging planned reuse and neglected targets for an author. “Due” would mean due under the chosen authoring rule—not scientifically measured forgetting or an individual learner's need. Authored appearances, actual encounters and successful retrieval are different quantities. Favor meaningful reuse in words/sentences; do not force unnatural prose to satisfy the sequence. The evidence does not require adaptive runtime scheduling.
3. **Revision story every 50 introduced characters:** A plausible editorial checkpoint, with no validated threshold of 50 in these sources. Inventory size alone ignores elapsed time, encounter frequency and difficulty. Such a story can complement ongoing reuse, but should not be the only opportunity to revisit earlier characters or evidence that they are mastered.
4. **Clicked-character cards → reread until no help:** A defensible practice loop, not a validated complete intervention. A card involves retrieval only if the learner attempts the relevant pronunciation/meaning **before** revealing the answer; simply viewing it is restudy. For orally fluent learners, the task is often linking a written form to a familiar spoken word, not learning a new meaning. Use enough word context to disambiguate readings/meanings. Story reading can itself involve retrieval; it is not necessarily passive restudy.
5. **No-help rereading is a local milestone, not a retention test.** Familiar passage memory/context can support success; clicking also measures help-seeking, not just memory failure. Retain rereading for meaningful practice, but assess durability separately through delayed unaided recognition and reading in new sentences/stories. Do not infer reading gains from handwriting or word-pair recall alone.

**Remaining uncertainty:** This scoped review found no strong direct trial of the complete proposed system in orally fluent, low-literacy Mandarin adults. It does not establish that none exists. If evaluating schedule choices, distinguish schedule shape from first gap, total practice span, review count and final-review-to-test delay; compare delayed new-context reading, not just immediate no-click completion.
